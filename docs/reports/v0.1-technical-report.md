# nebuRedaction v0.1 — technical report

**Status of this document.** nebuRedaction is an experimental project, and this
report is a blog-level write-up — at most an unreviewed preprint. It is written
to approach the standards of a rigorous publication: every number traces to a
recorded run, the gates were specified before they were measured, and the
failures are reported at the same length as the successes. It does not claim
the standard of a peer-reviewed venue, and it should not be read as one. Where
the best available source for a fact is a vendor's engineering blog, that
source is cited as what it is.

**Citations** follow APA 7th edition. Bibliographic details were checked against
each source on 2026-10-16; sources that could not be verified were not kept.

---

## 1. Abstract

nebuRedaction asks whether a credential detector small enough to run inline in a
logging pipeline — tens of kilobytes, microseconds per attribute, no
dependencies — can be obtained by distilling a general language model's
judgements into a tiny classifier trained on synthetic data. The engineering
works: a 89.4 KB gradient-boosted model reaches 0.804 recall at a 0.1%
false-positive rate, classifies an attribute in 4–19 µs, and its .NET and
Python implementations agree to within 6.661 × 10⁻¹⁶ on every class probability
of a 2,400-sample consistency set. The detection does not. The accuracy gate was
set at 0.834 recall and was not met; more importantly, the model learned the
*formats its generator produces* rather than the concept of a credential —
0.075% false positives on format families it trained on, 29.8% on families held
out, and worse than the rule baseline it was built to improve on when shown real
third-party code. **This project demonstrates a workable path and has not
reached a usable result.** Its contribution is the process and the measurements
that exposed that gap, not the model.

## 2. Problem definition

### 2.1 The detection unit

The unit of decision is one **structured-log attribute**, represented as the
tuple `(key, value, template, scope)`: the attribute's name, its value, the
message template it appeared in, and the scope or origin type that produced it.
A rendered message body is handled by cutting it into candidate fragments and
classifying each as an attribute, with the whole message as its template.

This choice is the project's central constraint. A detector that sees only the
value cannot use the fact that the attribute is called `password`; a detector
that sees the whole log line is no longer cheap. The tuple is the smallest unit
that carries both the value's shape and the naming context around it.

### 2.2 Classes

Seven: `none`, `password`, `token`, `api_key`, `private_key`,
`connection_string`, `cookie`. The six positive classes exist because the
redaction decision is not the only useful output — a downstream policy may
treat a session cookie differently from a private key — but the gate is binary,
and every reported operating point is "should this be redacted".

### 2.3 The cost asymmetry

A missed credential is a leak. A false positive destroys a log line that an
engineer needed. These are not symmetric, but neither dominates: a detector
that redacts aggressively is removed by the first team whose incident
investigation it obstructs, and a detector that never fires is decoration.

The project fixes the asymmetry by specifying a false-positive budget and
measuring recall at it. The operating point is **0.1%** false positives, with a
stricter **0.01%** profile offered for deployments where damaging logs is the
greater risk. Thresholds are chosen on a validation split and never on the
external test set.

### 2.4 Inherent ambiguity

Some units cannot be labelled correctly from the information available, and the
project treats this as a property of the problem rather than a defect in the
data. A 32-character hexadecimal string is a plausible API key and a plausible
MD5 digest. A UUID is a plausible session identifier and a plausible
correlation id. A 40-character base64 string under a neutral key name carries
no signal at all beyond entropy.

The synthetic generator therefore includes an `ambiguous` family group whose
members are, by construction, not decidable from the value: their label comes
from the key name and the template or not at all. The honest consequence is a
ceiling: no detector reading this unit can be perfect, and a report claiming
near-perfect accuracy on data containing these units is reporting an artefact
of its test set.

## 3. Related work and novelty

### 3.1 Knowledge distillation

Training a small model against a larger model's output distribution rather than
hard labels is due to Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015), who showed that the
"dark knowledge" in a teacher's full distribution transfers information that
one-hot labels discard. This project uses the technique directly, with one
deviation described in §5.3.

### 3.2 Weak supervision and programmatic labelling

Where hand labelling is infeasible, labels can be generated by programs whose
individual accuracy is poor but whose agreement structure is informative;
Snorkel (Ratner et al., 2017) is the canonical system. This project's labelling
pipeline is weak supervision in that tradition — a rule labeller with an
explicit confidence tier, a model teacher, and a human review of a sample — but
it does not learn a generative model over the sources' agreement. Its
combination rule is fixed in advance and stated, which is weaker and auditable.

### 3.3 Secret detection: tools and datasets

Production secret scanning is dominated by pattern matching. gitleaks (Rice,
n.d.) and detect-secrets (Yelp, n.d.) are the widely deployed representatives,
the first rule-driven, the second adding Shannon-entropy heuristics. Both are
used in this project — as controls in the evaluation, and as the gate that
keeps credentials out of this repository.

The machine-learning direction within secret detection is older than it is
often credited, and the closest prior work by *architecture* is Saha (2019),
whose thesis combines a regular-expression scan with a supervised classifier
specifically to **reduce the scanner's false positives** — which is exactly the
shape of this project's `rules+student` systems. It is worth setting out in
detail, because it is the one external result directly comparable to what this
project built:

| | Saha (2019) | This project |
|---|---|---|
| Architecture | Regular-expression scan, then a voting classifier (logistic regression, naive Bayes, SVM) over its hits | Rule patterns, then a distilled classifier, scored jointly |
| Features | 24, hand-designed | 31 dense, plus hashed n-grams |
| Unit | A candidate line from a repository scan | One structured-log attribute |
| Labelled data | ~5,000 examples from 300 repositories, hand-labelled | 240,000 generated units, plus 2,692 own-log units |
| Test set | 2,180 examples: 286 secrets, 1,894 false positives | 20,000 synthetic, 49,568 CredData, 2,692 own |
| Reported result | **recall 0.89, precision 0.84** | recall 0.804 at a fixed 0.1% false-positive rate |
| False positives at that point | **47 of 1,894 — a 2.48% rate** | 0.1% by construction of the operating point |

**The two recall figures are not in conflict, and the reason is the operating
point.** Saha's 0.89 recall sits at a 2.48% false-positive rate, which is
roughly twenty-five times more permissive than the 0.1% budget this project
measures at; her thesis removes more than 80% of the scanner's false positives,
which is a large improvement over the scanner and still far from a 0.1% budget.
Reporting the two numbers side by side without that column would be misleading.
The units and data differ as well, so neither result transfers to the other's
setting.

Three further points from that work bear on this one. It designed 24 features
by hand, a comparable undertaking to this project's 31, which suggests the
feature-engineering burden is intrinsic rather than a consequence of this
project's choices. It notes that as of 2019 **no public labelled dataset of
candidate secrets existed** — a gap CredData later filled, and the reason this
project has exactly one external test set. And it deliberately includes
**generic passwords**, which the large-scale GitHub leakage study it identifies
as closest to its own work had excluded on the grounds that passwords conform
to no distinct structure (Meli et al., 2019, as cited in Saha, 2019). This
project makes the same inclusive choice, and §2.4 is where that choice's cost
appears.

CredSweeper (Samsung, n.d.-b) implements a comparable regex-plus-model approach
and ships an optional model to filter rule hits; CredData (Samsung, n.d.-a) is
its companion dataset of credentials found in open-source repositories, and is
this project's only external, independently-constructed test set.

### 3.4 Sensitive information in software logs

The closest prior work by problem statement is SDLog (Aghili, Wu, Khomh & Li,
2025), a deep-learning framework for detecting sensitive information in
software logs, explicitly motivated by the poor generalisation of hand-written
regular expressions across log formats. The same group's empirical study of
sensitive information in logs (Aghili, Wu, Khomh & Li, 2024) surveys what
actually appears. SDLog reports 99.5% identification of sensitive attributes
and an F1 of 98.4% after fine-tuning on roughly 100 target-domain samples.

Two differences matter for reading this report. SDLog targets sensitive
information broadly — personal data, addresses, identifiers — while this
project targets **authentication credentials only**, a narrower and more
adversarial class. And SDLog is a deep-learning model fine-tuned per target
domain, whereas this project's deployment constraint is a dependency-free
library of tens of kilobytes running inline, which rules that architecture out
at inference time even where it would win on accuracy.

### 3.5 Training on synthetic data

The project's positives are generated, not collected, because collecting real
credentials at scale is not something one may responsibly do. The consequence —
that a model trained on generated formats may learn the generator — is a known
hazard, and §6.3 reports that it is exactly what happened here.

### 3.6 The universal weight subspace hypothesis, and why it is a different lever

Kaushik, Chaudhari, Vaidya, Chellappa and Yuille (2025/2026) report
large-scale evidence that trained networks converge into shared low-dimensional
spectral subspaces across initialisations, tasks and domains, studying over
1,200 models including 500 Mistral-7B LoRAs, 500 Vision Transformers and 50
LLaMA-8B models. A task can then be represented as a small coefficient vector
over a shared basis, with weights reconstructed as a mean plus a coefficient
combination of basis directions.

It is worth setting side by side with what this project does, because both are
described as "making models smaller" and they compress different things.

| | This project (distillation) | Universal weight subspace |
|---|---|---|
| What is compressed | The **runtime model itself** | The **task-specific delta** |
| What must be deployed | A 40–90 KB classifier, alone | A shared basis and mean, plus per-task coefficients — reconstructed into full weights |
| Cost it addresses | **Inference and deployment** | **Adaptation and storage** across many tasks |
| Prerequisite | A teacher that can be queried | A **large population of trained models of the same architecture** — on the order of 500 ViTs for a 16-dimensional subspace in the reported experiment |
| Result here | 40–90 KB, 4–19 µs, zero dependencies | Full base-model inference cost remains |

The paper presents itself as supplying the first large-scale empirical evidence
for the hypothesis, not as a closed result. Two details are worth stating
precisely, because they are easy to merge and they come from different
experiments:

- In the coefficient-only adaptation experiment, the paper's own framing is
  positive — more efficient and effective learning, with trainable parameters
  falling from 86 M to 10 K. **Its reported accuracies are nonetheless below
  full training on all five image-classification datasets**, by 1.6 to 8.7
  points. Its language-model comparison is against LoRA, not against full
  training.
- The larger out-of-distribution gap comes from a **different** experiment, in
  which weights are projected onto the subspace without training coefficients:
  0.3 points in-distribution against 3.5 points out-of-distribution.

### 3.7 Novelty

Searching the literature above, we found no published system that combines all
of: a credential detector specialised to structured-log attributes, distilled
from a general model into a sub-100 KB artefact, with a cross-language
bit-level equivalence gate between two independent implementations, and
generalisation measured against format families deliberately held out of
training.

That claim is deliberately narrow, and the novelty is **not the distillation**,
which is standard. It is the engineering and verifiability around it: the
format-family split as a generalisation probe, the `.nrm` container with a
reproducibility gate tying two implementations together to 10⁻¹⁶, and a
reporting discipline in which the gate that failed is reported as prominently
as the gates that passed. Whether that combination is *useful* is settled in
§6, and the answer there is no — which is itself the result.

## 4. Data

### 4.1 The synthetic generator

114 format families across the six positive classes and the negative class,
each carrying a provenance record: 43 derived from open standards, 35 from
vendor documentation, 27 declaring explicitly that they imitate no vendor
format and why, and 5 from a project's own repository. Values are generated
from a seed, so any corpus is reproducible byte for byte and none is committed
— the generator produces format-correct credentials, which any scanner must
treat as leaks.

Splits are 200,000 training units, 20,000 validation and 20,000 test.

### 4.2 Adversarial negatives

A detector that keys on the word "password" is not a detector. The generator
therefore constructs negatives under credential-suggesting carriers: a
non-secret value under a sensitive key name, under a sensitive template, or
both. The design was prompted by a measurement — in the project's first probe,
the teacher model labelled the *user name* in "Failed password for invalid user
`<name>`" as a password with probability 0.972, purely because the template
contained the word.

### 4.3 Held-out format families

Ten of the 114 families are excluded from training entirely, so that
generalisation to unseen formats is measurable rather than assumed. This is the
single most informative design decision in the project; §6.3 reports what it
caught.

### 4.4 Own logs

2,692 attribute units exported from nebuLog's own demonstration services. The
finding is itself worth reporting: **all 2,692 are negatives**, with eight
distinct key names, none credential-suggesting. They therefore contribute no
positives and cannot calibrate recall — but they are a real negative
distribution, and the false-positive rate is the half of the problem that
synthetic data calibrates worst.

### 4.5 CredData, and its 18.2% loss

CredData (Samsung, n.d.-a) is the only external, independently constructed test
set, used for **evaluation only** — never for training, threshold selection or
model choice.

Acquisition lost **12,387 of 67,896 annotations (18.2%)**: 11,425 to Windows
path-enumeration limits, 556 to filenames illegal on NTFS, 319 not fetched, and
87 to antivirus quarantine. The resulting set is 49,568 units. Re-running
acquisition under Linux was assessed as recoverable to within 0.13% in about two
hours and was **declined**, on the grounds that the project is a demonstration
and the recovery would require landing roughly 11 GB of repositories including
attack tooling on the development machine. The external test set this report
relies on is therefore missing nearly a fifth of its source annotations, and
every CredData number below should be read with that in mind.

### 4.6 Data security measures

Two are worth recording because both were forced by a control that fired.

**Nothing credential-shaped is committed.** An early attempt to commit the
fixture corpus was blocked by push protection with 33 findings. The options
were to bypass per secret, exclude the path from scanning, or stop committing
such fixtures; the third was chosen, costs nothing because generation is
deterministic, and keeps the control switched on. A manifest check stands
behind the one allowlisted path, requiring every credential-shaped fixture
under it to be a registered, generator-produced value.

**The scanner has a blind spot, and a test covers it.** Secret scanners
suppress values containing `EXAMPLE` or `TEST`, which is correct for a leak
scanner and leaves them unable to see a vendor's *published placeholder* — the
string most likely to be copied into a test. Three had accumulated here before
a provenance review found them. They are now generated at test time, and a
dedicated test enforces their absence, because the scanner structurally will
not.

## 5. Method

### 5.1 Evaluation first (gate G1)

The evaluation framework, test sets and metric definitions were built and frozen
**before** any model was trained, and the frozen sets are pinned by a lock file
recording each set's path, unit count and SHA-256. Changing a test set after
G1 requires declaring the change.

The rule baseline built at the same time is not a straw man: 27 value patterns,
each citing the document its format came from, plus key-name and template
classification. It establishes what the problem looks like without machine
learning, and it supplies the finding that shaped everything after it — **all
1,296 of the rule baseline's false positives trace to key names and templates**,
not to value shapes.

### 5.2 Teacher labelling

A general model was run over 157,256 units. The project's expectations of it
were wrong in an instructive way, and §6.1 reports the audit.

### 5.3 Distillation on positives only

Soft labels are used **only on units the generator labels positive**; negatives
are trained on hard labels alone. The reason is the audit result: the teacher's
agreement with the generator on negatives is 0.342, so its distribution over a
negative carries more noise than signal. Distilling it would teach the student
the teacher's false-positive behaviour, which is the behaviour the project most
needs to avoid.

The implementation detail worth stating: cross-entropy against a distribution is
identically a weighted sum of one-hot cross-entropies, so distillation is
implemented by sample replication with `sample_weight = p_k`. That is an
identity, not an approximation.

### 5.4 Three students

- **Model A** — multinomial logistic regression over hashed n-grams with L1
  regularisation and int8 quantisation. 41,274 B.
- **Model B** — gradient-boosted trees over 31 engineered dense features plus
  the 256 most frequent hash buckets. 91,512 B.
- **Model C** — a character-level CNN reading the value's characters only, no
  key name, no template. 40,092 B.

### 5.5 Calibration and operating points

Platt scaling, selected over isotonic regression by **recall at the operating
false-positive rate** rather than by Brier score — an isotonic fit that improves
calibration while creating score ties costs recall at a fixed false-positive
rate, and it also costs 263 KB against a 40 KB model. Two thresholds are
shipped, balanced (0.1%) and conservative (0.01%).

### 5.6 The `.nrm` container and gate G3

Weights ship in a private binary container: magic, format version, a UTF-8 JSON
metadata block, then a little-endian weight section. The format specifies two
things that a reimplementation would otherwise get subtly wrong — accumulate in
64-bit even though leaves are stored as 32-bit, and sum trees in file order,
because floating-point addition is not associative.

G3 requires the .NET library and the Python reference to agree to within 10⁻⁶
on every class probability, with identical decisions. §6.5 reports the margin.

## 6. Results

### 6.1 The teacher is much worse than expected

| measure | teacher | comparison |
|---|---:|---|
| Agreement with the generator on negatives | **0.342** | 65.8% of true negatives called credentials |
| False-positive rate on real own logs | **1.86%** (50 of 2,692) | CredSweeper: **0.00%** (0 of 2,692); target: 0.1% |
| `private_key` predictions | **33,708** | true `private_key` units: **813** |

Agreement is computed on "should this be redacted", not on the class, so 0.342
is not class confusion. Temperature scaling is monotone and cannot move an
argmax, so these rates are invariant under recalibration: 0.342 is a settled
number, not a provisional one.

This is the measurement that justified distilling on positives only, and it is
the reason the project's teacher-reliability audit is retained as a process
step rather than treated as a formality.

### 6.2 G2 is not met

The bar: recall ≥ 0.834 at 0.1% false positives, model ≤ 100 KB.

| system | recall @0.1% | size | meets G2 |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| **`rules+student-b`** | **0.804** | 91,512 B | no |
| `student-b` | 0.783 | 91,512 B | no |
| `rules+student-c` | 0.751 | 40,092 B | no |
| `student-c` | 0.726 | 40,092 B | no |
| `rules+student-a` | 0.687 | 41,274 B | no |
| `student-a` | 0.654 | 41,274 B | no |
| `rules+entropy` | 0.611 | — | no |

Three points short. **Size was never the difficulty** — A and C use about 40% of
the budget, B 87%.

The plan expected model A to be the default and model C an upper-bound
reference. The measurement reverses that order, and §6.4 explains why.

### 6.3 The students learned the generator, not the concept

This is the project's main finding. Split by whether the format family was seen
in training, at the same threshold:

| slice | units | A recall | A FPR | B recall | B FPR | C recall | C FPR |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| seen families | 18,327 | 0.697 | **0.075%** | 0.830 | 0.084% | 0.744 | 0.084% |
| held-out families | 1,673 | 0.212 | **29.8%** | 0.306 | 48.9% | 0.539 | 42.4% |

**On formats they have seen, all three models sit inside the 0.1%
false-positive budget.** Every bit of the overall excess comes from the 1,673
held-out units, where a third to half of all negatives are flagged. The models
are not miscalibrated; they are being asked about formats they have never seen.

Average precision says the same from the other direction: the student ranks
held-out families at 0.436, the worst of all systems measured, while
`rules+entropy` ranks them at 0.961, the best. Entropy is format-agnostic; a
hashed n-gram model is not.

On CredData — real credentials in real repositories — the collapse is complete:

| system | recall | FPR | AP |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `rules+student-a` | 0.172 | 23.3% | 0.282 |
| `student-a` | 0.077 | 22.9% | 0.214 |
| `rules+entropy` | 0.162 | 1.22% | 0.709 |
| `rules` | 0.503 | 5.96% | 0.341 |
| `credsweeper` | 0.807 | 1.21% | 0.723 |

The student is beaten by everything, including the rule baseline it was built to
improve on.

These are not three failures. They are one: **the student learned the
generator's formats, not the concept of a credential.** It is excellent inside
the distribution — 0.972 average precision, 0.075% false positives on seen
families, 0.00% on real nebuLog logs — and poor the moment the format is new.
The held-out families were constructed to detect exactly this, and the earlier
baseline report predicted it in advance: the value patterns and the generator's
positive families derive from the same documents, so on synthetic data a
detector is tested against its own specification.

### 6.4 Why model C wins where it matters

Model C reads the value's characters and nothing else — no key name, no
template, no hashed n-grams — and it generalises far better to held-out
families (0.539 recall against A's 0.212) while being the smallest of the
three.

The comparison that isolates the cause is A against B. B has **more** capacity
and reads **fewer** hash buckets — 256 selected by frequency, against A's full
32,768-dimensional hashed space — and it is better almost everywhere. So the
overfitting is not a property of the model class. **It is the hashed n-gram
space itself**: a feature that fires on a specific 4-gram of a specific
generated format is a lookup table for that format.

### 6.5 G3 is met, with ten orders of magnitude to spare

| | |
|---|---|
| Samples | 2,400 — 1,000 synthetic, 1,000 own logs, 200 adversarial, 200 robustness-transformed |
| Probability comparisons | 16,800 |
| Tolerance | 1 × 10⁻⁶ |
| **Worst absolute difference** | **6.661 × 10⁻¹⁶** |
| Decisions | identical under both profiles |

The margin is the point. 6.661 × 10⁻¹⁶ is about 3 ULP at a probability near 1 —
not "close enough" but the same arithmetic in the same order on the same bytes.
The tolerance is nowhere near load-bearing: a genuine divergence is a feature or
an operation differing, which appears thousands of times larger, not a rounding
error creeping past 10⁻⁶.

The gate passed on its first run, which is a weak signal on its own — a
comparison that silently compares nothing also passes first time. It was
therefore re-run against a deliberately perturbed copy of the set, with one
probability moved by 10⁻⁵ and one decision flipped; it failed, naming the
sample and the class, and passed again on the real set.

### 6.6 Performance

Measured with BenchmarkDotNet on an Intel Core Ultra 5 245K, .NET 10.0.12,
Windows 11, in Release:

| input | mean | P95 | allocated |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2-character value | 4.2 µs | 4.3 µs | 5,608 B |
| 32-character hex | 6.1 µs | 6.1 µs | 6,584 B |
| 512-character value | 18.5 µs | 18.8 µs | 5,504 B |
| mask a value | 129 ns | 130 ns | 192 B |

Against the 20 µs p99 target: the two shorter inputs are comfortably inside at
every percentile measured. **p99 itself was not measured** — the harness
exposes percentiles only to P95, and a p99 estimated from the iteration count a
4 µs method needs would mostly estimate its own noise. The 512-character case
at 18.8 µs P95 sits close enough to 20 µs that its p99 may well exceed it, and
this report does not claim otherwise.

The first measurement of that case was 70.7 µs and 202,568 B. The cause was not
the trees: feature extraction built a substring, a namespaced string and a
UTF-8 byte array for each of 2,042 n-grams to fill a 32,768-bucket map, from
which the model then read 256 buckets. Hashing each n-gram in place and
accumulating only into buckets the model reads is exact — the hashed features
are not normalised, so a bucket nothing reads cannot affect one that is — and
costs 3.8× less time and 37× less memory. **The near-zero-allocation
requirement is still not met** at 5.5 KB per call.

## 7. Methodology: a reusable process for hardening a capability

The model is not the transferable part of this project. The process is, and it
is written here as something another team could run against a different narrow
task.

### 7.1 The eight stages

| Stage | What it means | What it was here | Reusable artefact |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Define the unit** | Fix the smallest thing a decision is made about, and what context it carries | `(key, value, template, scope)`; seven classes; a stated cost asymmetry and a false-positive budget | A unit schema with a version, and a written statement of which errors cost what |
| **2. Data governance** | Decide what data may be used for what, **before** acquiring any | Third-party data for evaluation only, enforced by a loader that raises on third-party paths; nothing credential-shaped committed | A licence review per source; a loader that makes the policy unbypassable rather than advisory |
| **3. Construct the data** | Generate what cannot be collected, and build the probes that will expose over-fitting | 114 provenanced families; adversarial negatives under credential-suggesting carriers; **ten families held out** | A seeded generator; a per-family provenance field; a held-out split by *format family*, not by row |
| **4. Evaluation first** | Build and freeze the evaluation before any model exists | Frozen test sets with a lock file; a non-trivial rule baseline; gate G1 | `testsets.lock`; a baseline strong enough to be embarrassing |
| **5. Teacher labelling** | Obtain labels at scale from a general model, and **audit the teacher** | 157,256 units; audit found 0.342 agreement on negatives | A teacher-reliability audit as a routine step, not a formality |
| **6. Train and select students** | Several model classes, selection rule fixed before the numbers are seen | A, B, C; selection by recall at the operating point; calibration chosen the same way | A selection rule written into the trainer before the grid runs |
| **7. Ship and verify across languages** | A format, two implementations, and a gate binding them | `.nrm` v1; gate G3 at 10⁻⁶, measured at 10⁻¹⁶ | A container spec that states the arithmetic; a per-sample equivalence gate |
| **8. Report honestly** | Report the gate that failed as prominently as the ones that passed | G2 not met, stated in the abstract, the package descriptions and the model card | A reporting rule: no number without a run behind it |

### 7.2 When this process applies

It is not general. The conditions under which it is worth running:

1. **The task boundary is narrow.** "Is this attribute a credential" qualifies;
   "is this log line sensitive" is already broader than what a 40 KB model can
   hold.
2. **The input is short and structured.** The unit here is a handful of fields,
   not a document.
3. **The judgement rests mainly on local features.** If the answer depends on
   state elsewhere in the system, a per-unit classifier is the wrong shape.
4. **Enough data can be constructed or obtained.** Here it could be generated,
   which is what made the project possible and also what limited it.
5. **A test set exists that is independent of the training data.** Without one,
   §7.3's first risk is undetectable, and the project would have reported 0.972
   average precision and called it a success.

### 7.3 The three risks that actually bite

**Risk 1 — the student learns a shortcut rather than the capability.** This is
the project's main finding and the one most likely to go unnoticed, because a
shortcut scores *well* on data drawn the same way. It is detectable only by
evaluating on something constructed differently: held-out format families
(0.075% → 29.8% false positives) and external real data (recall 0.172 against
the rule baseline's 0.503). **Budget for these probes at the start**; they
cannot be retrofitted credibly after a result is in hand.

**Risk 2 — the gap between synthetic and real distributions.** Synthetic data
fixes the positives' shapes and, more quietly, their *variety*. Real
repositories contain credential forms the generator never imagined, and real
logs contain negatives far stranger than any generated negative.

**Risk 3 — thresholds do not transfer across distributions.** Measured here: a
threshold selected for 0.1% false positives on the synthetic validation split
measured **1.223% on CredData — twenty times its target** — with recall
collapsing from 0.611 to 0.162. A threshold is a property of a distribution,
not of a model. Any deployment on a new distribution must re-select it, and
without labels on that distribution it cannot.

### 7.4 Choosing a teacher is a cost-quality trade, and should be stated as one

This project chose a model it could run locally at low cost. That choice
bought unlimited labelling at no marginal cost and a teacher whose agreement
with the generator on negatives is 0.342. **That number characterises this
choice, not teachers in general**, and nothing here should be read as evidence
about other models. A frontier model would almost certainly label better and
would have made 157,256 calls a budget question instead of an afternoon.

What survives the choice is the **audit**: measuring the teacher against the
labels you already trust is cheap, it is what revealed that negatives were
unusable for distillation, and it should be a step in the process whichever
teacher is used.

## 8. Limitations and future research

### 8.1 Candidates not compared

Time, not evidence, ruled these out. None was tested and rejected:

- Morphological abstraction with a linear model.
- Separate key-name and value models with a late fusion.
- Character language-model surprisal as a feature.
- One-class or anomaly detection — attractive here precisely because negatives
  are abundant and positives are the constructed class.
- A small MLP, and a GRU over characters.
- A quantised model C.

Given §6.4 — that the hashed n-gram space is where over-fitting lives, and that
the value-only model generalises best — the character-level candidates are the
ones most likely to pay.

### 8.2 Known gaps in what was done

- **Model C was not ported, tuned or calibrated.** It generalises best and
  shipped nowhere.
- **Robustness transforms were never run against the students**, only against
  the rule baselines, where base64 wrapping defeated everything.
- **Near-zero allocation is not met** (5.5 KB per call) and **p99 was not
  measured** (§6.6).
- **G3 runs locally only.** The trained model and the corpus are deliberately
  not committed, so continuous integration can verify reader parity against a
  581-byte hand-built fixture but cannot run the real 2,400-sample gate.
- **The `cookie` class has no external data.** CredData has no corresponding
  category, so `cookie` is only ever tested on synthetic and own data.
- **LogHub and SDLog are unused** pending licence decisions, so the project has
  no real log corpus with positives in it.
- **The external test set is missing 18.2% of its source annotations** (§4.5).

### 8.3 The limitation users must be told about

This is not a caveat about the method; it is an operational constraint on the
shipped artefact.

The model reads **256 of 32,768 hash buckets, selected by frequency over the
training corpus.** Anything that changes the corpus — further training, adding
target key names or vocabulary entries, substituting one's own logs — may
change *which* 256 buckets are selected, and therefore produces a different
model.

**Adding a target word is consequently not a matter of appending a line to a
vocabulary.** It requires, in order: retrain → recalibrate (the Platt
parameters belong to the model) → re-choose both thresholds → re-run the
evaluation. None of the four is skippable, and §7.3's third risk makes the
third especially unsafe to skip.

Every number in this report holds for **one model, one corpus and one test
set.** G2's 0.804 moves when any of them does. G3's 6.661 × 10⁻¹⁶ is a property
of two implementations rather than of the data, but it must be re-verified for
any new model.

For the model as shipped, the converse also holds and is worth stating:
measurement shows the vendor key-name tokens are **not** among the 256 buckets
it reads, so adding or renaming such key names does not change its output. The
limitation bites after retraining, not before.

### 8.4 Future research: adapt the teacher in a subspace, then distil

The weakest link in this project is the teacher (§6.1). The subspace work in
§3.6 suggests a cheap way to improve it: rather than fine-tuning a teacher
fully, adapt it to log redaction by learning only a small coefficient vector
over the shared subspace of its architecture, train that on synthetic data, and
distil the improved teacher through the pipeline described here.

The attraction is that it attacks the right link. The teacher's 0.342 agreement
on negatives is what forced distillation onto positives only, and a teacher
that is merely *reliable on negatives* would unlock the half of the training
signal currently discarded.

Three things must be verified before it is worth attempting:

1. **Can enough same-architecture trained models be obtained** to extract a
   subspace? The reported experiment used roughly 500 ViTs for a 16-dimensional
   subspace, and this project's teacher is not a model class with 500 public
   siblings of the same architecture.
2. **Does adapted-teacher agreement on negatives actually rise** from 0.342?
   This is measurable with the audit already built, at low cost, and it is the
   gate the idea should pass before anything is distilled.
3. **Do students distilled from it improve on held-out families and
   CredData** — the two slices where this project failed? An improvement in
   teacher agreement that does not move those is not worth the complexity.

A further extension: if user-retrained versions of this project's own models
fall inside a shared subspace, a customised detector could be distributed as
"base model plus coefficients" rather than as a whole model. That is
speculative, and the measurement in §8.3 — that retraining reselects which
buckets are read — suggests the subspace of a *hashed-feature* model may be far
less well-behaved than that of a dense network.

## 9. Reproducibility

### 9.1 Seeds and determinism

Every generated artefact is deterministic from a recorded seed. The synthetic
corpus is generated from the configuration and seed rather than committed; the
consistency set is drawn with a fixed seed; the fixture model is hand-specified
and committed whole.

### 9.2 Commands

```bash
# Synthetic corpus
uv run --group datagen python -m datagen.generate \
  --config datagen/configs/v0.yaml --seed 20261011 --size 500 \
  --out <dir> --manifest <manifest.json>
uv run --group datagen python -m datagen.validate <dir>/train.jsonl \
  <dir>/val.jsonl <dir>/test.jsonl

# Evaluation
uv run --group eval python -m eval.run

# Export and verify the shipped model
uv run --group train python training/export_nrm.py

# G3 consistency set, then the .NET side
uv run python tools/generate_consistency_set.py
NEBUREDACTION_REQUIRE_CONSISTENCY_SET=1 dotnet test imady.NebuRedaction.slnx -c Release

# Latency and allocation
dotnet run --project dotnet/benchmarks/imady.NebuRedaction.Benchmarks -c Release -- --filter '*'
```

### 9.3 Frozen test sets

`eval/testsets.lock` records each test set's path, unit count and SHA-256. A
report computed against different data is detectable by comparing against it,
and changing a set after G1 must be declared.

### 9.4 Environment

Python 3.12 with dependencies locked in `uv.lock`; .NET 10.0.12 targeting
`net10.0` and `netstandard2.1`; measurements on an Intel Core Ultra 5 245K
under Windows 11. The shipping libraries have **zero runtime dependencies**,
verified at the package level rather than the project file.

### 9.5 What cannot be reproduced from this repository

The trained model weights and the corpora are not committed. A reader can
regenerate the corpus from the seed and retrain, but will not reproduce this
report's exact numbers without the same model artefact, which is held back
pending final verification.

### A note on two sources

**Saha (2019) is now cited from the full text**, which Frank supplied, and
every figure attributed to it in §3.3 was read there: the 2,180-example test
set of 286 secrets and 1,894 false positives, the 47 remaining false positives,
the reported 0.89 recall and 0.84 precision, the 24 features, the 300
repositories and the ~5,000 labelled examples. The 2.48% false-positive rate in
the comparison table is **derived** — 47 of 1,894 — and is marked as such
rather than attributed to the thesis. As a check on the reading, the thesis's
reported precision reproduces from its own counts: 0.89 × 286 true positives
against 47 false positives gives 84.4%.

This replaces an earlier, weaker entry. The work had first been located through
CredSweeper's architecture documentation, which cites a **2020 conference
version** of it, co-authored with the thesis committee; that record is at
`https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020coms.conf...30S` and is **not
independently verified** here, so this report cites the thesis it has read
rather than the conference paper it has not. The earlier draft also carried a
page range, pp. 168–175, taken from a secondary source and contradicted by the
conference record's own identifier, which encodes page 30. Neither was
confirmable and the entry now needs neither.

**Meli et al. (2019)** is a secondary citation and marked as one: the
bibliographic entry above is transcribed from Saha's own reference list, which
carries the full author list, title, venue and link, so the entry is accurate —
but **this report has not read that work**, and makes no claim about its
contents beyond the single attribution in §3.3, which is itself what Saha
reports about it.

A process failure is worth recording here, because it is the third of its kind
in this work order. I first wrote this entry's authors and title **from memory**
and only then checked the thesis's bibliography. They happened to match. That
is luck, not method: the same habit produced a page range of pp. 168–175 that
was wrong, and a merged claim about two different experiments in §3.6 that the
source does not support. The rule that caught all three is the same one —
check the original before writing the sentence, not after.
